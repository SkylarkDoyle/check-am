How to check if a drug or medicine is NAFDAC registered

A registry lookup is one useful step before you buy a medicine. It is not medical advice, and it never replaces a pharmacist.

Key points

  • CheckAm is not medical advice. Ask a pharmacist or a doctor about any medicine.
  • Look for the NAFDAC number on the pack and look it up in the registry.
  • Compare the product name and the maker with the pack.
  • Check the batch number, the expiry date and the seal.
  • Buy from licensed pharmacies, not from roadside sellers or hawkers.

A word first about safety

CheckAm is not medical advice and cannot tell you whether a medicine is right for you, safe for a child, safe in pregnancy or safe with other medicines. Only a doctor or a pharmacist can do that. This guide is about one thing: looking up what the NAFDAC public registry says about a product, so that you notice a mismatch before you pay.

This guide is mainly about over-the-counter medicines, the ones you can buy without a prescription. For anything prescribed, follow what your doctor and your pharmacist tell you.

Step 1: Find the NAFDAC number on the pack

Look on the outer carton and on the inner pack, such as the blister strip or the bottle label. The number is printed after the words "NAFDAC Reg. No." or "NAFDAC No.". It is usually a prefix and digits, for example A4-2260 or 01-4721.

If you cannot see a number, that is worth noting before you buy. Also write down the product name, the strength if it is shown, and the name of the maker.

Step 2: Look it up

In CheckAm, type the number or the product name, or take a photo of the pack and let the app read the number. If the app misreads a character, correct it and check again.

The result shows what the NAFDAC public registry has on record: the product, the company and the registration status. Medicine names can be similar, and one brand can come in several forms and strengths, so read the entry carefully.

Step 3: Compare the result with the pack

  • Does the product name match the pack?
  • Is the maker or the company the same as the one printed on the pack?
  • Do the form and strength look consistent, for example syrup or tablet, if the entry shows them?

A number that is on record for a different product or a different company is a mismatch. Do not buy the product, and ask the pharmacist about it. If you already have it, do not use it until you have spoken to a pharmacist or a doctor.

Step 4: Check the pack itself

The registry cannot see your pack, so look at it carefully:

  • Batch number and dates. The batch number and the expiry date should be printed clearly. Do not buy anything that is expired.
  • Seal and carton. A torn, re-stuck or opened carton is a warning sign.
  • Print and spelling. Look for blurry print, spelling mistakes and colours that look wrong.
  • The medicine itself. Tablets that are chipped, damaged or different in colour from the rest of the strip, or a liquid that looks cloudy or has changed colour, are reasons to stop.
  • The leaflet. Many medicines come with an information leaflet. Check that it is there and readable.

For more on these checks, see how to check if a product is fake in Nigeria.

Where you buy matters

Buy medicines from a licensed pharmacy. A pharmacy has a pharmacist who can answer your questions, and it is a fixed place you can go back to. Medicines sold from a bag, a roadside table or a moving vehicle are harder to trust, because you do not know how they were stored or where they came from.

Ask the pharmacist to confirm that the product suits you, and tell them about any other medicine you take. Keep the receipt.

What a registry check cannot tell you

A registry match means that the number is on record and shows what it is on record for. It does not tell you that a pack is genuine, that the medicine inside is what the label says, or that it is right for you. CheckAm shows what the registry says. It does not decide whether a product is genuine.

That is why the pharmacist, the seal, the dates and where you buy all matter.

If you are unsure or feel unwell

  • Do not buy it, and stop using it if you already have it.
  • Keep the pack, the batch number and your receipt.
  • If you feel unwell after taking a medicine, get help from a doctor, a pharmacist or a health centre.
  • Report the product to NAFDAC through the contact details on its official website.

Frequently asked questions

Is CheckAm medical advice?

No. CheckAm only shows what the NAFDAC public registry says about a name or number. Ask a pharmacist or a doctor about any medicine.

Can I check a drug by name?

Yes. You can type the name in CheckAm. Names can be similar and one brand can have several forms, so compare the entry with the pack.

Does a registry match mean the medicine is genuine and safe?

No. It means the number is on record. It does not prove the pack or the medicine is genuine, and it cannot say whether the medicine suits you.

Where should I buy medicines?

Buy from a licensed pharmacy, where you can ask a pharmacist your questions and keep a receipt.
